Quarterstone

A two-player online co-op action RPG seen at 45°. Worlds are endless and seeded: the generator lays features out in whole metres on a 1 m grid, then builds every surface from 25 cm voxels — the metre decides what a thing is, the quarter-metre decides how it looks. Columns are stored as spans rather than heights, so a rim can hang over your head and a tunnel can open onto its face. Five biomes blend into one another across a continuous climate field. Grass and water are never voxelised, and never still.

Concept images · one generator, six seeds

What the same rules produce somewhere else

Each plate below is a single 32 × 32 m chunk, drawn by the generator from the seed printed under it. No hand placement: the seed picks the climate, the climate picks the palette, the props and the feature set, and the 25 cm layer roughens everything afterwards.

World rules

Whole metres for shape, quarter metres for character

The generator is only allowed to size features in whole metres, so everything stays legible and traversable: a 3 m ledge is always exactly three 1 m steps. A second pass then displaces the surface by ±2 voxels, which is where the silhouette gets its grain.

One chunk: 32 × 32 m footprint, 16 m ceiling, 128 × 128 × 64 voxels. Chunks stream in and out around both players; the seed makes every neighbour reproducible, so co-op partners generate identical terrain without sending it.

FeatureMetrics — whole metresRule

Canyon

3, 4 or 5 m wide · 3–4 m deep

Cut after the heightfield, floor snapped to a metre. Walls expose strata one voxel band at a time; ledges are legal footing.

River

1–4 m wide · bed 1 m below bank

Follows the terrain downhill and drops in whole-metre steps, so every elevation change becomes a fall or a cascade.

Hills

1–3 m rise

The default ground state, sampled per metre. Over 45° the surface switches from soil to rock so the palette states the traversal rule.

Arc

4–6 m span · 3–5 m rise

Placed where a canyon or river crosses a rock-heavy cell. Bridge above, covered fight below.

Column

2–4 m tall · 1–2 m across

Basalt in the barrens, hoodoos on the mesa. Vertical cover that reads instantly at iso distance.

Detail pass

±2 voxels (±0.5 m)

The only sub-metre operation. Chips edges, rounds boulders, scatters shelf rubble — the whole reason the world doesn't look like a spreadsheet.

Overhangin build

1–2 m undercut · 2–3 m gap

Rims undercut where the drop is 2 m or more. Facing overhangs narrow a 5 m canyon to a gap you can clear on foot — the first reason to look up in an isometric game.

Waterfallin build

1 m per step

Emitted automatically wherever a watercourse steps down a metre. Falling sheet, lip foam, and churn in the pool below; no authoring.

Pondin build

1.25 m deep

Basins that sit below the local mean fill and hold. Deeper than a river, so the depth ramp in the water shader has something to read.

Cavein build

1 m cells · opens on faces

Cut from the column before anything else runs. Mostly hidden under an isometric camera — which is the point: you find them by their mouths in a canyon wall.

Talusin build

+1 m at cliff feet

The erosion pass raises ground under a 3 m drop and cuts a metre off banks standing high over water. Cliffs stop meeting the floor at a right angle.

Trailin build

3 m wide · steps ≤ 1 m

Routed with A* over the movement costs, then the ground is graded under it so no step exceeds a metre and the shoulders are pulled in. Packed earth, grass worn off, and fences, lamps and crossings placed along it.

Landmarkin build

9–12 m tall

One per region on the highest flat ground: obelisk, hive tree, wrecked machine or standing stones. Navigation for two players without a map.

Screein build

1–2 voxel chips

Derived, not scattered: every drop of 2 m or more sheds rubble at its base, in the rock colour of the cell above it.

Grass

0.3–0.9 m blades

Not voxels at all. Two sine bands of wind plus a trample radius around every character, biome-tinted and biome-dense.

Traversability

What a player can cross, and proving the world allows it

Whole-metre features exist so that movement can be stated in whole metres too. The budget below is the contract between the generator and the character controller: if the generator produces something outside it, the world is wrong, not the player.

MoveBudgetNote

Step up

1 m

Free, and it is the grid unit. Every authored feature height is a whole number of these.

Vault

2 m

Costs momentum and puts your hands on the ledge. The ceiling for climbing without gear.

Jump gap

2.5 m

Paired overhangs across a canyon are tuned to leave exactly this — a gap that looks impossible and isn't.

Drop

6 m

Survivable. Past that it costs health, which is what the 16 m ceiling is really measuring.

Wade

0.75 m

River beds sit exactly here, so a river is always crossable on foot and a pond at 1.25 m is not.

Swim

deeper

Slow, no attacks, and your partner reaches the far bank before you do.

Magma

lethal

Impassable at infinite cost, so no route, bridge or built thing is ever placed across it.

The reach pass

After the world is built, a flood fill runs from the spawn using exactly the budget on the left — step, vault, the two-metre hop, wade — and marks every cell it can get to. Arm Reach on the diorama and everything it could not reach turns red.

It is a generator test rather than a debug view. A mesa top that comes back red is not a bug in the overlay: it means the erosion pass left no ramp, and the fix belongs in generation. Ledges, talus and undercut rims exist partly to keep that from happening.

Isolated ground is not automatically wrong — an island in a river or a shelf reachable only by hookline is content. What the pass catches is the case nobody meant: a site, a landmark or a crossing that no player can get to.

Next: repair rather than report — when a site comes back unreachable, cut a ramp to it in whole metres and re-run the fill until it passes.

Dressing

Clutter, structures and points of interest

Everything here is voxelised on the same 25 cm lattice as the terrain, so it chips, topples and bridges under the same rules. Natural clutter is derived from the terrain wherever it can be — scree comes from the drop above it, stumps from where a trunk fell — because scattered dressing reads as scattered dressing.

Systems

Eight systems, now in the generator

These were the proposals on the last plate, and they are all running in the render above. Each replaced a pile of special cases with one rule the generator applies everywhere — which is why the clutter list stopped growing while the world kept getting denser. Caves are cut at 1 m resolution and columns hold at most a handful of spans; both are budgets, not limits of the model.

Biomes

A climate field, not a tile map

Two low-frequency fields — temperature and moisture — are sampled per metre. Each biome sits at a point in that space and every column is a weighted blend of all five, so palettes, densities and feature sets cross-fade instead of switching. There is no boundary to stand on.

The climate space, sampled on a hex lattice. Each cell is the blend a column at that temperature and moisture would receive; the five markers are the biome anchors.

Gear system · frames

Eight frames, each a hex lattice

A weapon is a frame: base stats, an affinity, and a cluster of hexagonal sockets. Frames never level up. Seven of the eight are symbiotic rather than historical — grown, spliced, planted or magnetised — and each was designed around a discipline instead of around a silhouette. The longblade stays as the control case, because a set of oddities needs one plain thing to be measured against.

Adjacency

Two modules fuse only when their hexes share an edge. A hex has six neighbours, so a centre socket can anchor up to six fusions at once — the densest builds are the hardest to assemble.

Shape is a stat

A line of three and a triangle of three hold the same modules and produce different weapons. Layout is the build, not decoration.

Affinity

Seat two modules of a frame's own discipline and its trait wakes up. A bio frame fed bio modules starts behaving like the thing it was grown from — which is not always what you wanted.

Armour shares the lattice

Cuirass, greaves and helm frames use the same hexes and the same nine modules, so a fusion learned on a weapon transfers.

Gear system · modules

Three disciplines, nine modules

Tech is machined, finite and precise. Magic is hungry, reactive and cheap to feed. Biological is slow, alive and it changes while you carry it. Any module fits any socket.

Gear system · fusions

Thirty-six pairings, and what they do to each other

Every adjacent pair resolves. Same-discipline pairs amplify a stat and take a drawback; cross-discipline pairs bend behaviour into something neither module does alone. The named fusions below are the ones worth building a frame around.

Status: pre-production. This plate locked the generation rules, biome set and gear lattice; the terrain generator it draws with is now a module the game shares, and a character controller moves through what it generates under the movement budget above. Still ahead: a camera, netcode and Steam integration (appid 480, Spacewar, for development). The plate remains a design document, not a prototype of the engine — its instanced-box renderer is explicitly not the shipping approach.
